# What the repeat-tract fits cost, and why the bench said five hours

**Status:** research report, 2026-10-14, branch `str-fit-cost` (from `str-fit-compare` at c5f91e1d).
It answers the cost questions the head-to-head comparison left open
([`compare_B_D_2026-10-13.md`](compare_B_D_2026-10-13.md) §7): why the bench's estimate for kimura was
far above the real run, what bounds the memory, and whether the fits meet the research plan's budget
([`../str_fit_research_plan.md`](../str_fit_research_plan.md) §6: the fit at kimura's scale, 2,169
samples, within one hour on one machine with 64 GiB).

The methods are the comparison's: **B**, the counting fit class by class with F read off pairs of
samples and sandwich standard errors; **D**, penalised counting; **`D_tract_prior`**, D with B's prior
on the allele-length shares.

---

## 1. The answer

**The bench's estimate was high for six reasons, and none of them is that the bench's rows cost more
than real ones.** On the same machine and code, a real row of tomato2 cost about what a drawn row cost
(1.2 against 2.0 CPU-milliseconds for B with its errors, §3). The estimate of five hours for B came out
about 19 times the real kimura run (300 minutes against 15.5). Six measured factors, multiplied,
account for 14 to 18 of the 19:

| what the estimate assumed | what kimura's run had | factor | measured on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 178 million sample-locus rows with reads | 55 to 72 million: the repeat-tract guard dropped 45,432 of 86,688 tracts, and about 80% of samples, not 95%, have a read at a kept tract | 2.5 to 3.2 | kimura's logs and files (§3.1) |
| the shipped ±9 allele window | ±6 | 1.7 | tomato2, the fit alone: 0.75 against 0.45 ms a row |
| the fit with its standard errors | no standard errors | 1.4 | the bench's 2,000 × 3 cell: 3,620 against 2,616 CPU-seconds |
| the bench's truth: 158 rounds of counting | tomato2's real classes: 90 rounds | 1.8 | both, rounds weighted by rows |
| the bench's reads: 1.36 read lengths a row | tomato2: 1.09 | 1.15 | one round of counting: 8.1 against 7.0 µs a row |
| the cost a row growing with loci a class (1.72 against 1.43 ms) | — | 1.2 | the growth came from the pairs' table and is gone with it (§4) |

The remaining factor of 1.1 to 1.4 is kimura's machine (96 cores against this Mac's 18 threads) and
kimura's own rounds, which its log does not report; the two cannot be separated from here. kimura's
processor model is not recorded anywhere in the project, so no per-core factor is given.

**Two changes, prototyped here, cut the fits' cost 20 to 50 times and their memory 3 to 20 times,
with every fitted number unchanged to better than one part in a million** (§4, §5):

1. **F off pairs of samples without a table of pairs.** With the allele-length shares held, as every
   use holds them, a pair of samples' likelihood is a ratio of two cubics in the spread. Each pair is
   reduced to its cubic's four numbers and the table of 2.9 kB a row is never built. The bench's
   largest memory cell (one-base runs, 2,000 samples, 400 loci a class) fell from **21.6 GB to 1.1 GB**
   for B and from 3.5 to 1.0 GB for D.
2. **Each distinct row of reads scored once.** A sample's reads at a tract enter a round of counting
   only through how many reads fall at each length and through the sample's homozygote excess. The
   costly part, every genotype's likelihood of the reads, depends on the reads alone. On tomato2,
   754,601 rows hold 8,780 distinct ones, class by class; on the bench's 2,000 samples, 1.85 million
   hold 27,046. So each distinct row is scored once and each sample adds three numbers to its row's
   totals.

**After both, the budget is met by all three methods on CPU time, with a wide margin**, at the bench's
own count of rows:

| method | CPU a row, µs (largest measured) | at 178 million rows, on this Mac | at kimura's 55 to 72 million |
|---|---|---|---|
| B, with its errors | 37 to 69 | 6 to 12 minutes | 2 to 5 minutes |
| D | 33 to 96 | 6 to 17 minutes | 2 to 7 minutes |
| `D_tract_prior` | 31 to 53 | 5 to 9 minutes | 2 to 4 minutes |

"On this Mac" divides the CPU time by 17, the speed-up the comparison measured on its 18 threads
before these changes. **That division is the weak point**: the machine carried a load of 60 to 120
throughout this work (another agent's grid), so no wall time here means anything, and how well the
new code spreads over 18 threads was not measured. If it spread no better than over 4 threads, the
largest figure (D at 178 million rows) would be 71 minutes. A quiet run is needed to replace the estimate with a time.

**Memory at kimura's scale** is now the evidence itself plus under a gigabyte: about 24 GiB at 178
million rows (the evidence holds 139 bytes a row on tomato2, 142 in the bench's draw), about 10 GiB at
kimura's actual rows. The comparison's estimate was 51 GiB.

**No further lever is needed for the budget.** The ones measured, for the record (§6): a looser
stopping tolerance saves 12% of rounds; the ±6 window halves the cost but loses the long tracts it was
widened for; the standard errors are now about half of B's cost.

## 2. How it was measured

**Every time is CPU time**, the process's user and system seconds over all its threads, read from the
process CPU clock or `/usr/bin/time -l`. CPU time does not count waiting for a core, so it is the
measure that survives a shared machine. It is not untouched by load: on the bench's 2,000 × 3 cell, B
with its errors took 2,636 CPU-seconds quiet (the comparison's run) and 3,620 here, at a load of about
85. **Every CPU time below was taken at a load of 60 to 120 on 18 cores and is about 1.4 times what a
quiet machine gives.** Ratios between runs at the same load are sound; absolute figures are high.
The load never fell below 3, so no wall time was taken.

**`examples/str_fit_cost_probe.rs`** (added here) loads one cohort's classes, real from its censuses or
drawn from the bench's truth, and fits them one class after another as the real driver
`ng_str_counting_fit` does. For each class it prints the evidence's shape (rows with reads, reads a row,
read lengths with reads a row, distinct rows) and the CPU time of one round of counting, one reading
of F off pairs of samples, the whole fit with its count of rounds and readings, and the sandwich
errors. `--numbers` writes every fitted number and error at full precision, so two builds can be
compared number by number. D and `D_tract_prior` are fitted per motif length, as the bench fits them.

**The cohorts:**

- **tomato2**, real: 59 accessions over 32 Mb, the psp files the counting fit was developed on
  (`../pop_var_caller-stutter-multiplier/tmp/route2/psps_tomato2_32mb`), homozygote excess 0.98 for
  every sample; 45 classes with at least 8 tracts, 754,601 rows with reads.
- **The bench's 2,000 × 3 cell**: the tomato-like PCR truth, 2,000 samples, 3 reads, at most 100 loci a
  class, seed 1; one-base runs (1.85 million rows) and two-base runs (1.67 million).
- **The bench's memory cell**: the same at 400 loci a class, one-base runs (5.1 million rows), run
  through `ng_str_fit_bench` with the held-out score off, as the comparison ran it.

Each run was taken alone among this study's own jobs at 2,000 samples, with free memory logged every
30 seconds and a stop below 8 GiB; free memory never fell below 29 GiB.

## 3. The gap between the bench and the real run

### 3.1 kimura's rows

The real run's log lines that count rows were not copied off kimura, so the count is read three
ways from what was:

- **The genotype rows** written from the same evidence: 46 GB over 34 classes, a row being 740 bytes at
  ±6 (91 genotypes of 8 bytes, a tract index and an excess): 62 to 67 million rows.
- **The largest class's likelihood table** in the older fit: 5.9 GiB at 728 bytes a row, 8.7 million
  rows for at most 5,000 tracts: about 1,740 samples with reads a tract, 80% of 2,169. Times the 41,256
  tracts the guard kept: up to 72 million.
- **The evidence's resident memory**, 7.1 GiB once read (`spec/fit_precision.md` §7), at tomato2's 139
  bytes a row: 55 million.

So kimura's fit saw **55 to 72 million rows**, where the comparison assumed 178 million. Two
assumptions differ: every selected tract kept (the guard dropped 52% of them on kimura, a rate that
grows with the number of samples and is flagged as an open question in `spec/fit_precision.md` §7),
and 95% of samples read at a tract (about 80% on kimura). If the guard is fixed to keep the tracts,
kimura's rows will be about 150 million, so the budget below is given at the comparison's 178 million
as well.

### 3.2 A real row against a drawn one

Before either change, on the same machine and load:

| | tomato2, real | bench, 2,000 × 3, one-base |
|---|---|---|
| reads a row | 3.85 | 3.16 |
| read lengths with reads, a row | 1.09 | 1.36 |
| rounds of counting, weighted by rows | 90 | 158 |
| one round of counting, µs a row | 7.0 | 8.1 |
| one reading of F off pairs, µs a row | 116 | 118 |
| readings of F | 2 | 2 |
| the fit, ms a row | 0.75 | 1.42 |
| the errors, ms a row | 0.46 | 0.54 |

**A drawn row costs 1.15 times a real one a round, and the bench's truth takes 1.8 times the rounds.**
Nothing else differs. The bench counts rows the same way the driver does (`rows_with_reads`), and
its timing included the sandwich errors but not the held-out score.

**The pairs' table was 27% of the fit on real data and 15% on drawn data**: one reading of F cost as
much as 15 to 17 rounds of counting, because the spread's search scores every pair 61 times over the
19 × 19 table.

**The window**, on tomato2 before the changes: the fit cost 0.45 ms a row at ±6 and 0.75 at ±9 (one
round 3.9 against 7.0 µs a row: 91 genotypes against 190).

### 3.3 Threads

The driver fits classes one after another, each spreading its tracts over the pool; the bench's B
fitted every class at once. That changes when memory is held (§4) but not the CPU time. How the time
spreads over 18 threads could not be measured under this load.

## 4. The memory: F off pairs of samples as a cubic

**The table.** To read F off pairs of samples, each sample with reads at a tract held its likelihood
of every ordered pair of allele lengths: 19 × 19 numbers of 8 bytes, 2.9 kB a row. The search for the
spread then scored every pair of neighbouring samples 61 times from these tables.

**The change.** With the shares held, a pair's likelihood depends on the spread `α` only through the
urn's weights `α · share`, and every term of it is a polynomial in `α` over `α(α + 1)(α + 2)(α + 3)`.
Put over one denominator it is `N(α) / ((α + 1)(α + 2)(α + 3))` with `N` a cubic whose four
coefficients are sums of products of likelihoods, shares and excesses, none negative. So each pair is
reduced to four numbers while its tract's rows are built, and the search evaluates a cubic. The
derivation is in the doc comment of `PairCubic` (`composite_fit.rs`); a test checks the cubic against
the closed-form likelihood to 10⁻¹² at 19 lengths, spreads from 10⁻³ to 10⁴ and every excess.

Every use holds the shares while the spread moves: B's search, D's table of F's likelihood, and both
methods' standard errors (each point of the sandwich builds its own pairs). `SamplePairs::of` now takes
the shares; its scorers no longer do.

**Peaks**, the whole process, drawn cohort included:

| run | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| bench, one-base, 2,000 × 3, 400 loci a class, B | 21.6 GB | 1.1 GB (cubic) / 1.6 GB (both changes) |
| the same, D | 3.5 GB | 1.0 GB / 1.3 GB |
| the same, `D_tract_prior` | — | 1.3 GB |
| the probe, bench 2,000 × 3, 100 loci, B | 2.0 GB | 0.49 GB |
| the probe, tomato2, B, D and `D_tract_prior` | 2.8 GB | 0.41 GB |

The drawn cohort alone holds 0.73 GB at 400 loci a class. With both changes the fit adds a distinct-row
index of 16 bytes a row (§5) and 32 bytes a pair of samples, against 2.9 kB before; the bench's B, which
fits all 14 classes at once, holds them for every class together, which is why its peak is 0.5 GB above
the cubic alone.

**The numbers did not move.** Every number B fitted on tomato2 at ±9 is within 4 × 10⁻⁷ of itself
(relative), the errors within 7 × 10⁻⁷, and every class took the same number of rounds. On the bench's
2,000 × 3 cell, within 2.4 × 10⁻⁷. Differences of this size come from the search for the spread landing
a hair apart and the counting then stopping at its tolerance of 10⁻⁷; they are a hundred thousand times
smaller than the standard errors.

**Time.** One reading of F fell from 116 to 6.2 µs a row on tomato2 (19 times), and B's fit from 564
to 402 CPU-seconds there. The errors rose 8% (343 to 372 s), since each point now builds its own pairs
instead of reusing a table.

**The cost a row no longer grows with the loci a class.** Before, B cost 1.43 ms a row at 100 loci a
class and 1.72 at 400 (the comparison, quiet). With the cubic alone, at this load, it cost 1.79 ms a
row at 100 loci (the probe) and 1.70 at 400 (the bench), both with errors: the growth went with the
tables. Why the larger tables cost more a row was not measured.

## 5. The time: each distinct row of reads scored once

**Why it is exact.** In a round of counting, a sample's reads at a tract enter through two things: its
row of reads (how many reads at each length, its libraries pooled) and its homozygote excess. The
excess only decides how the row's genotype likelihoods are mixed: the posterior of each genotype is
`(c₁ · one_g + c₂ · two_g) / T`, where `one_g` and `two_g` are the genotype's prior times its likelihood
for the sample's copies as one draw and as two, `c₁` and `c₂` the chances of each given the excess, and
`T` the sample's total. Every count the round gathers is a sum of these posteriors times terms of the
row. So the samples holding one row need only three sums, `Σ c₁/T`, `Σ c₂/T` and `Σ (from the tract)/T`;
the genotypes are scored once per distinct row.

**How many distinct rows.** At about three reads a sample, most reads report one length, and the same
few patterns recur:

| cohort | rows with reads | distinct rows, class by class | distinct rows, tract by tract |
|---|---|---|---|
| tomato2, 59 samples | 754,601 | 8,780 | 189,069 |
| bench, 2,000 × 3, one-base | 1,845,379 | 27,046 | 136,398 |
| bench, 2,000 × 3, two-base | 1,668,546 | 26,195 | 100,495 |

The rounds of the fit use the class's distinct rows. The standard errors need counts tract by tract,
so they group a tract's samples by row. The reading of F off pairs scores each distinct row's table
once and takes each distinct pair of rows' sums once.

**The numbers did not move.** Against the original code, on tomato2 at ±9: B's numbers within
4 × 10⁻⁷, its errors within 7 × 10⁻⁷, the rounds identical; D's and `D_tract_prior`'s numbers within
4 × 10⁻⁶; their errors within 10⁻⁵ except D's on the 6-base runs, a motif length of 1,396 rows in
all, where 63 of its errors moved by up to 2 parts in 1,000 (the largest a share's error of
1.774 × 10⁻⁵ becoming 1.777 × 10⁻⁵). On the bench's 2,000 × 3 one-base cell, B within
4 × 10⁻⁷. A test checks the grouped counts against the sample-by-sample ones, tract by tract and over
the class, to 10⁻¹¹ on a drawn class whose samples carry different excesses.

**Time, original code against both changes**, CPU-seconds at the same load:

| run | B fit | B errors | B in all | D | `D_tract_prior` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| tomato2, ±9 | 564 → 14.7 | 343 → 30.0 | 908 → 45 (20×) | 1,383 → 54 (26×) | 821 → 40 (21×) |
| tomato2, ±6 | 337 → 9.3 | 147 → 15.9 | 484 → 25 (19×) | — → 18 | — → 18 |
| bench 2,000 × 3, one-base | 2,616 → 60.5 | 1,004 → 65.6 | 3,620 → 126 (29×) | — → 105 | — → 92 |
| bench 2,000 × 3, two-base | | | — → 116 | — → 160 | — → 84 |
| bench, 400 loci a class, whole process | | | 8,748 (quiet) → 188 (47×) | 8,366 (quiet) → 169 (50×) | → 157 |

Where no "before" is given, the original was not run at this load. In the last row the "before" is the
comparison's quiet run, so the true ratio is about 1.4 times larger still.

**A round of counting now costs 0.2 to 0.26 µs a row**, against 7 to 8 before; most of what is left
is each sample adding its three numbers. The cost a row falls as classes grow, because the distinct
rows grow more slowly than the rows: 69 µs a row for B at 100 loci a class, 37 at 400. The largest
classes at kimura's scale hold 5,000 loci, so the cost a row there should be lower still; the table in
§1 uses the measured figures, not that expectation.

**What remains of B's time**: the errors (0.9 to 2 times the fit), the rounds, and the two readings of
F. D on real data runs its classes in step, a motif length at a time, until the slowest settles: 717
rounds a class on tomato2's one-base runs against B's 90 (track 2 §10.1 described this). It no longer
matters much: D costs 1.2 to 1.4 times B on these runs.

**Building the distinct rows** is spread over the threads in blocks of 64 tracts, merged in order, so
the rows are numbered as one pass would number them and the answer does not depend on the number of
threads. Every sum over rows is taken in fixed chunks added in order, for the same reason. Rebuilt this way, B's every number and error on tomato2 at ±9 came out identical to the bit to the
one-pass build; `D_tract_prior`'s moved by at most 2 × 10⁻¹⁰, which is likely the penalised fit's own
sums over tracts, taken in the order threads finish (not checked by running it twice).

## 7. Not done, and uncertain

- **No wall time.** The machine's load stayed between 60 and 120. Whether the new code spreads over 18
  threads as the old one did (17 times) is the one assumption between the CPU figures and the budget.
- **kimura's rows** are read from three indirect figures (§3.1), not from its log.
- **kimura's rounds** are not known: its output reports only the last counting's rounds. A real cohort
  of 2,169 samples may need more rounds than tomato2's 59.
- **Distinct rows at kimura's scale** were not measured; at 5,000 loci a class and higher depth they
  will be more than the bench's 27,000, and the cost a row will rise towards what it was if most rows
  become unique (at 30 reads or more a sample, say). The bench's 2,000 × 3 cell is the only
  large-cohort measurement here, and it is at three reads.
- **Tests:** the `ssr_fit` tests pass (113, two of them new: the cubic against the closed form, the
  distinct rows against sample by sample).

## 9. Addendum, 2026-10-14: wall time on a quiet machine, and deeper reads

**Status:** the same branch with `str-fit-compare` (6c81a0ab) merged in, both changes adopted. The
machine was quiet: every run started with the 1-minute load below 3, one process at a time, nothing
else running; free memory never fell below 38 GiB. Every run is `str_fit_cost_probe` with `--no-units`
(the whole fit only), classes one after another as the driver fits them, B with its sandwich errors.
"Wall" is the fit's own time, without loading the cohort.

### 9.1 The answer

**At three and ten reads a sample all three methods fit kimura's scale within the hour, even at
178 million rows. At thirty reads, B does not at 178 million rows (about 72 minutes); D and
`D_tract_prior` do.** Minutes on this Mac, from the measured wall time a row (the largest of the cells
measured at that depth, so an upper figure for larger classes, §9.4):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | B | 8.0 | 7 | 10 | 24 |
| 3 | D | 9.6 | 9 | 12 | 28 |
| 3 | `D_tract_prior` | 7.7 | 7 | 9 | 23 |
| 10 | B | 10.5 | 10 | 13 | 31 |
| 10 | D | 9.7 | 9 | 12 | 29 |
| 10 | `D_tract_prior` | 4.4 | 4 | 5 | 13 |
| 30 | B | 24.3 | 22 | 29 | **72** |
| 30 | D | 14.2 | 13 | 17 | 42 |

The 3-read figures are tomato2's (59 real samples, small classes, the slowest a row); the drawn

**Memory at thirty reads is the new risk, for D.** When reads stop repeating, the work per distinct
row returns, and so does its memory: D held 4.8 GB at 2,000 × 30 (1.9 million rows) against 0.8 GB at
2,000 × 3, because it scores every class of a motif length side by side and each class keeps its
distinct rows' genotype columns during a round. Scaled by distinct rows that would not fit kimura at
30 reads; fitting D's classes' rounds one at a time, as B does, would bound it. B peaked at 1.1 GB
there.

### 9.3 Threads

**CPU time divided by wall time overstates the threads doing work on a quiet machine**: the thread
pool's idle workers spin before they sleep, and that spinning is counted as CPU time. B's CPU time on
the 400-loci cell is 84 s on one thread and 144 s on 18, for the same work. So the speed-up is read
from wall times, one-base, 2,000 × 3, 400 loci a class:

| threads | B, wall s | speed-up | `D_tract_prior`, wall s | speed-up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 83.7 | 1.0 | 71.8 | 1.0 |
| 4 | 26.8 | 3.1 | 20.3 | 3.5 |
| 9 | 17.9 | 4.7 | 11.1 | 6.5 |
| 18 | 15.2 | 5.5 | 7.4 | 9.7 |

This Mac's 18 threads are 6 fast cores and 12 slower ones, so no method reaches 18. B scales less
than D because the driver fits its classes one after another and a small class cannot fill the pool;
fitting B's classes side by side, as the bench does, would close some of that.
